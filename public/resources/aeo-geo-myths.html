AEO & GEO Myths: 76 Fact-Checked Claims

76 common AEO, GEO and AI search claims checked against official documentation and research, with practical advice for SMEs.

By Mushbloom · Published 12 October 2026

Crawlability & technical signals

Files, crawler access, rendering and speed: what AI systems actually need to reach your pages.

“You need an llms.txt file to appear in AI answers.” — Debunked

Reality: llms.txt is a community proposal. No major AI search provider has documented it as a requirement or ranking input. Pages without it are cited every day.

What to do: Add one if it is cheap — it is a tidy summary for agents and developers — but prioritise crawlable, substantive pages first.

Source: llms.txt proposal

“Blocking GPTBot removes you from ChatGPT search results.” — Nuanced

Reality: OpenAI documents separate agents: GPTBot (training), OAI-SearchBot (search) and ChatGPT-User (user-triggered fetches). Blocking one does not block the others.

What to do: Decide per crawler. Many businesses allow search and user agents while choosing a training policy separately.

Source: OpenAI — Overview of OpenAI crawlers

“Blocking Google-Extended removes you from Google AI Overviews.” — Debunked

Reality: Google states Google-Extended controls use of content for Gemini model training and grounding in certain products, not inclusion in Search or AI Overviews, which follow Googlebot.

What to do: Keep Googlebot allowed if you want Search visibility; treat Google-Extended as a separate data-use decision.

Source: Google — Overview of Google crawlers (incl. Google-Extended)

“AI crawlers render JavaScript like Googlebot does.” — Debunked

Reality: Googlebot renders JavaScript, but many AI fetchers retrieve raw HTML only. A client-only app can look empty to them.

What to do: Serve meaningful HTML: server rendering, prerendering or static summaries of key pages.

Source: Google Search Central — JavaScript SEO basics

“Single-page apps cannot rank at all.” — Debunked

Reality: Google can index JavaScript sites; the risk is delay, rendering errors and invisibility to non-rendering bots, not an absolute ban.

What to do: Test pages with URL Inspection and a no-JS fetch; prerender the pages that matter commercially.

Source: Google Search Central — JavaScript SEO basics

“There is a special robots.txt directive that boosts AI citations.” — Debunked

Reality: robots.txt only controls crawling permissions. It cannot request preference, priority or citation.

What to do: Use robots.txt to allow the crawlers you want and block private areas — nothing more.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to robots.txt

“Robots.txt noindex hides pages from search.” — Debunked

Reality: Google does not support noindex in robots.txt. A blocked URL can still be indexed from links, without its content.

What to do: Use a noindex meta tag or header on a crawlable page to keep it out of the index.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to robots.txt

“Submitting a sitemap guarantees indexing.” — Debunked

Reality: Sitemaps aid discovery. Engines still choose what to crawl and index based on quality and demand.

What to do: Keep the sitemap clean, then strengthen internal links and content on unindexed pages.

Source: Bing Webmaster Guidelines

“IndexNow makes Google index pages faster.” — Debunked

Reality: IndexNow is supported by Bing, Yandex and others. Google does not participate.

What to do: Use IndexNow for Bing (which also powers Copilot grounding) and Search Console for Google.

Source: IndexNow documentation

“Page speed is a major factor in being cited by AI.” — Nuanced

Reality: Speed is a modest classic-search signal, and very slow or timing-out pages may fail to be fetched. There is no evidence of a strong citation reward for speed alone.

What to do: Fix timeouts and severe slowness; do not chase perfect scores at the cost of content work.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

Structured data & schema

What markup does and does not do for AI answers and classic search features.

“Schema markup makes ChatGPT cite you.” — Debunked

Reality: No major LLM provider documents schema as a citation trigger. Generation works from retrieved text; markup may help indexing systems understand entities.

What to do: Use accurate schema for search features and entity clarity, not as a citation lever.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

“FAQ schema still produces rich results for most sites.” — Debunked

Reality: Google limited FAQ rich results to well-known authoritative government and health sites in 2023.

What to do: Write visible FAQs for users; do not expect FAQ snippets in Google for a typical business.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

“There is special schema required for AI Overviews.” — Debunked

Reality: Google states no special markup or optimisation is required for AI features beyond normal Search eligibility.

What to do: Focus on indexable, helpful pages that meet Search Essentials.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

“Structured data is useless for AI search.” — Nuanced

Reality: It is not a direct citation lever, but it helps search systems understand organisations, products and authors, which feeds knowledge systems that AI products may rely on.

What to do: Mark up Organization, Product, Article and LocalBusiness accurately where they genuinely apply.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

“More schema types on a page always helps.” — Debunked

Reality: Markup must describe visible content. Irrelevant or misleading markup can trigger manual actions.

What to do: Mark up only what the page really shows; validate with the Rich Results Test.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“JSON-LD is preferred over microdata by Google.” — Verified

Reality: Google recommends JSON-LD as the easiest to implement and maintain, while supporting other formats.

What to do: Use JSON-LD in new builds.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

“Schema can replace missing on-page content.” — Debunked

Reality: Structured data describes page content; it does not substitute for it. Hidden or unsupported claims violate guidelines.

What to do: Put facts in visible text first, then mark them up.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

“sameAs links help connect your brand entity.” — Verified

Reality: sameAs pointing to official profiles is a standard way to disambiguate an organisation in structured data.

What to do: Link only profiles you control and keep names consistent across them.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

“Review schema on your own homepage creates star ratings.” — Debunked

Reality: Google does not show self-serving review stars for LocalBusiness and Organization pages that host reviews about themselves.

What to do: Collect reviews on independent platforms; use Product review markup only where eligible.

Source: Google Search Central — Introduction to structured data

Content format, freshness & volume

Which content qualities make a page usable as an answer source, and which volume habits waste money.

“Publishing more articles is the fastest path to AI visibility.” — Debunked

Reality: Mass-produced, low-value pages risk scaled-content spam policies and add little that retrieval systems prefer.

What to do: Publish fewer, better pages that answer real buyer questions with specifics.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“AI-written content is penalised by Google.” — Debunked

Reality: Google evaluates quality and intent, not authorship method. Scaled low-value content is penalised however it is produced.

What to do: Use AI as a drafting aid with expert editing, original detail and fact-checking.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Changing the date on a page makes it “fresh”.” — Debunked

Reality: Updating dates without substantive changes is called out by Google as not helpful and can mislead users.

What to do: Update dates only when content materially changes.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Adding statistics and citations can increase visibility in generative answers.” — Nuanced

Reality: The GEO study found quotations, statistics and source citations improved visibility in its test setup. Results varied by domain and engine and are not universal guarantees.

What to do: Add genuine, sourced data where it helps readers — never invented figures.

Source: Aggarwal et al. — GEO: Generative Engine Optimization (arXiv 2311.09735)

“Keyword stuffing works on LLMs because they read words.” — Debunked

Reality: The GEO study found keyword stuffing performed poorly, and it remains a spam policy violation in search.

What to do: Write naturally; cover the concepts and questions buyers ask.

Source: Aggarwal et al. — GEO: Generative Engine Optimization (arXiv 2311.09735)

“Long-form content always beats short content.” — Debunked

Reality: Length is not a ranking or citation factor in itself. Completeness for the query matters.

What to do: Make pages as long as the answer requires and no longer.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Clear headings and direct answers make content easier to reuse.” — Verified

Reality: Retrieval systems split pages into passages. Self-contained, well-labelled sections are easier to match and quote accurately.

What to do: Lead sections with a direct answer, then supporting detail.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“You must write in Q&A format for AEO.” — Nuanced

Reality: Questions as headings can help match queries, but forcing every page into Q&A harms readability.

What to do: Use questions where buyers genuinely ask them; use normal structure elsewhere.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Hidden text for bots improves AI understanding.” — Debunked

Reality: Showing bots different content from users is cloaking and violates spam policies.

What to do: Make machine-readable content visible or available to users too (for example a plain-text edition).

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“Original first-hand experience matters for quality.” — Verified

Reality: Google’s helpful-content guidance explicitly asks whether content shows first-hand experience and expertise.

What to do: Include real process detail, photos, examples and named authors.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“AI answers only quote pages ranking in the top 3.” — Debunked

Reality: AI features often draw from a wider set of sources than the top organic results, including via query fan-out.

What to do: Cover sub-questions thoroughly; a page can be cited without ranking first.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

“Translating pages automatically creates new visibility markets.” — Nuanced

Reality: Localised content can reach new audiences, but unreviewed machine translation at scale risks low quality.

What to do: Translate priority pages with human review and proper hreflang.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

Links, mentions & off-site signals

How backlinks, brand mentions, reviews and third-party corroboration relate to AI visibility.

“Backlinks no longer matter in the AI era.” — Debunked

Reality: Links still help discovery and authority in classic search, which many AI systems use for retrieval.

What to do: Earn links from relevant, reputable sites through useful assets and partnerships.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“Unlinked brand mentions can support AI visibility.” — Nuanced

Reality: LLMs learn from and retrieve text where your brand is discussed, link or not. The effect is plausible but hard to measure directly.

What to do: Pursue genuine mentions in industry media, podcasts and communities.

Source: Aggarwal et al. — GEO: Generative Engine Optimization (arXiv 2311.09735)

“Buying links is a safe shortcut.” — Debunked

Reality: Link schemes violate spam policies and can lead to manual actions or ignored links.

What to do: Invest in digital PR and assets worth citing instead.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“Reddit and forum discussions influence AI answers.” — Nuanced

Reality: Some AI products frequently surface community content, but presence and impact vary by product and topic.

What to do: Participate honestly where your buyers already discuss problems; never astroturf.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“Press releases alone build authority.” — Debunked

Reality: Syndicated releases are usually nofollow and duplicated. Value comes from journalists who pick up the story.

What to do: Use releases for genuine news and pitch editors directly.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“Third-party reviews help buyers and machines trust a business.” — Verified

Reality: Independent reviews provide corroborating evidence that people and systems can weigh.

What to do: Ask satisfied clients for reviews on reputable platforms.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Guest posting on any site helps.” — Debunked

Reality: Large-scale guest posting with keyword links is treated as a link scheme.

What to do: Contribute occasionally to relevant publications for audience, not anchors.

Source: Google Search Central — Spam policies

“Being listed in industry directories is worthless.” — Nuanced

Reality: Spammy directories add nothing; reputable, curated industry lists provide corroboration.

What to do: Choose a handful of respected directories relevant to your sector.

Source: Bing Webmaster Guidelines

“Internal links help engines find and understand pages.” — Verified

Reality: Internal links are a primary discovery path and signal relative importance.

What to do: Link from strong pages to new or unindexed ones with descriptive text.

Source: Bing Webmaster Guidelines

Brand authority & entities

Entity clarity, consistency and the evidence that makes a business recognisable to machines.

“You need a Wikipedia page to be cited by AI.” — Debunked

Reality: Wikipedia helps notable entities, but countless businesses are cited without one, and promotional pages get deleted.

What to do: Build verifiable third-party coverage first; notability follows.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Consistent business information across the web helps entity understanding.” — Verified

Reality: Conflicting names, addresses or descriptions make it harder for systems to resolve which entity is meant.

What to do: Standardise name, description, contact details and profiles.

Source: Bing Webmaster Guidelines

“A clear “about” page and named authors support trust.” — Verified

Reality: Google’s guidance asks whether it is clear who created content and why they are qualified.

What to do: Publish real author bios, credentials and company background.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“E-E-A-T is a direct ranking score.” — Debunked

Reality: E-E-A-T is a quality-rater framework, not a single algorithmic score.

What to do: Demonstrate experience and trust in content; do not chase a mythical score.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Small businesses cannot compete with big brands in AI answers.” — Nuanced

Reality: Big brands have advantages on broad queries, but niche, local and specific questions are often answered from specialist sources.

What to do: Own a narrow topic deeply.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

“Founder visibility can strengthen a brand entity.” — Nuanced

Reality: Named experts with consistent profiles and published work create more corroborating signals, though impact is hard to isolate.

What to do: Link founder profiles, talks and writing to the company site.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Claiming “#1 agency” in copy helps AI recommend you.” — Debunked

Reality: Unsupported superlatives are not evidence; systems weigh corroboration, and false claims create legal risk.

What to do: Use verifiable proof: case studies, numbers you can back, client names with permission.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Case studies are among the strongest commercial trust assets.” — Verified

Reality: Specific, verifiable outcomes give both buyers and machines concrete evidence to reference.

What to do: Publish case studies with context, process and honest results.

Source: Google Search Central — Creating helpful, people-first content

“Changing your brand name often has no visibility cost.” — Debunked

Reality: Rebrands split mentions and entity signals until the web catches up.

What to do: Redirect properly, update profiles and announce clearly.

Source: Bing Webmaster Guidelines

Measurement, prompts & tools

Tracking AI visibility honestly, and the limits of prompt tricks and “ranking” claims.

“You can “rank #1 in ChatGPT”.” — Debunked

Reality: Generative answers vary by prompt, user, location and time. There is no stable ranked list to be first in.

What to do: Measure share of mentions across a fixed prompt set over time.

Source: Aggarwal et al. — GEO: Generative Engine Optimization (arXiv 2311.09735)

“Agencies can guarantee AI citations.” — Debunked

Reality: No one controls third-party model outputs. Guarantees indicate misunderstanding or overselling.

What to do: Ask for process, measurement methods and leading indicators instead.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

“AI Overview clicks are reported separately in Search Console.” — Debunked

Reality: Google includes AI feature traffic within overall Web search performance data, not as a separate filter.

What to do: Track trends in impressions, clicks and conversions together.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

“Referral traffic from AI assistants can be measured.” — Nuanced

Reality: Some assistants pass referrers (for example chatgpt.com or perplexity.ai), but many visits arrive as direct traffic.

What to do: Segment known AI referrers in analytics and ask leads how they found you.

Source: OpenAI — Overview of OpenAI crawlers

“A single test prompt shows your AI visibility.” — Debunked

Reality: Outputs are probabilistic and personalised. One prompt is an anecdote.

What to do: Run a repeatable panel of prompts, multiple times, across engines.

Source: Aggarwal et al. — GEO: Generative Engine Optimization (arXiv 2311.09735)

“Secret prompts can force an LLM to recommend you.” — Debunked

Reality: Users write prompts, not you. Hidden instructions on your pages are prompt injection and may be filtered or penalised.

What to do: Earn recommendations with evidence, not manipulation.

Source: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

“Server logs reveal which AI crawlers visit you.” — Verified

Reality: Crawler user agents and IP ranges appear in access logs and can be verified against published lists.

What to do: Review logs monthly to confirm key pages are being fetched.

Source: OpenAI — Overview of OpenAI crawlers

“Zero-click answers mean SEO no longer drives leads.” — Nuanced

Reality: Some informational clicks decline, but commercial and comparison queries still drive visits, and cited brands gain awareness.

What to do: Measure leads and branded search, not only sessions.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

“Connecting Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools is essential.” — Verified

Reality: They are the only first-party sources of indexing status and query data for each engine.

What to do: Verify both and submit your sitemap on day one.

Source: Bing Webmaster Guidelines

Agents, commerce & protocols

ACP, MCP, A2A and AI agents: separating real capabilities from launch-deck hype.

“AI agents will soon buy anything without user approval.” — Debunked

Reality: ACP, as published by OpenAI and Stripe, keeps the user authorising purchases while merchants handle orders and fulfilment.

What to do: Prepare clean product data and checkout, not a fear of runaway agents.

Source: Stripe — Developing an open standard for agentic commerce

“ACP replaces your payment processor.” — Debunked

Reality: ACP is a protocol for agent checkout flows; payments still run through supported processors and the merchant remains merchant of record.

What to do: Check your platform and processor support before planning work.

Source: Stripe — Developing an open standard for agentic commerce

“MCP gives AI unrestricted access to your systems.” — Debunked

Reality: MCP standardises how applications connect to tools and data. Access depends on the servers and permissions you configure.

What to do: Expose only scoped tools, with authentication and logging.

Source: Model Context Protocol — Introduction

“MCP is just another name for an API.” — Nuanced

Reality: MCP servers usually wrap APIs, but MCP defines a common interface so many AI apps can discover and call tools in the same way.

What to do: Use MCP when several AI clients need the same tools; a direct API is fine for one integration.

Source: Model Context Protocol — Introduction

“AI agents are ready to run whole businesses unsupervised.” — Debunked

Reality: Agents still make errors, are vulnerable to prompt injection and need oversight for consequential actions.

What to do: Start with supervised workflows and human approval steps.

Source: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

“Prompt injection is a real security risk for agents.” — Verified

Reality: OWASP lists prompt injection as the top risk for LLM applications.

What to do: Separate untrusted content from instructions and limit tool permissions.

Source: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

“Product feeds will matter for agentic shopping.” — Verified

Reality: Agentic checkout relies on structured, accurate product data that agents can read and act on.

What to do: Keep titles, prices, availability and policies accurate and machine-readable.

Source: Stripe — Developing an open standard for agentic commerce

“Small businesses should wait until agents are mainstream.” — Nuanced

Reality: Full agentic commerce is early, but the groundwork — clean data, clear policies, automated intake — pays off now.

What to do: Automate repetitive admin today; adopt protocols when your platform supports them.

Source: Model Context Protocol — Introduction

“A chatbot on your site makes you “AI-ready”.” — Debunked

Reality: A widget does not make your content crawlable, your data structured or your workflows automated.

What to do: Fix discoverability and processes before adding chat.

Source: Google Search Central — AI features and your website

RAG, embeddings & LLM mechanics

How retrieval and generation work, and the myths that come from misunderstanding them.

“LLMs search the live web for every answer.” — Debunked

Reality: Some products retrieve live results for some queries; others answer from training data. Behaviour varies by product, mode and query.

What to do: Be present both in long-lived web sources and in crawlable current pages.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“RAG eliminates hallucinations.” — Debunked

Reality: Retrieval grounds answers in sources and reduces some errors, but models can still misread or misattribute retrieved text.

What to do: Keep human review for important outputs and cite sources.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“Content is retrieved in passages, not whole pages.” — Verified

Reality: Retrieval systems typically split documents into chunks and match the most relevant ones to a query.

What to do: Make each section understandable on its own.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“Vector search makes keywords irrelevant.” — Debunked

Reality: Many production systems combine semantic and keyword (hybrid) retrieval because exact terms still matter.

What to do: Use the real names of products, places and problems.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“Fine-tuning a model is the best way to add company knowledge.” — Debunked

Reality: For changing facts, retrieval is usually cheaper and easier to update; fine-tuning better suits style and task behaviour.

What to do: Start with a knowledge base plus retrieval.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“Training cut-offs mean new businesses are invisible to AI.” — Nuanced

Reality: Base models may not know you, but retrieval-enabled products can find current pages.

What to do: Make sure current pages are crawlable and corroborated.

Source: OpenAI — Overview of OpenAI crawlers

“Bigger context windows remove the need for retrieval.” — Debunked

Reality: Long contexts cost more and models can miss details in long inputs; retrieval still selects what matters.

What to do: Feed focused, relevant material.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“LLMs cite the original source of every fact.” — Debunked

Reality: Citations point to retrieved pages, which may not be the original source, and some answers carry no citation.

What to do: Publish original data clearly attributed so others cite you.

Source: Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (arXiv 2005.11401)

“Opting out of training stops all AI use of your content.” — Nuanced

Reality: Training opt-outs do not necessarily block search or user-triggered retrieval, which use different agents.

What to do: Review each crawler’s documented purpose.

Source: OpenAI — Overview of OpenAI crawlers
